# Give Mochi ears first, then a voice

> **Status:** study only, nothing built yet (26 October 2026). Written from four research notes on
> Apple's on-device speech, cloud voice APIs, local models and wake words, and voice UX; the raw
> notes stay in the local `research_notes/` folder.
>
> **Team lead's note:** this report assumes Mochi and Spark are pose pictures, so it prices talking
> frames and lip-sync as new art for each character. They are being rebuilt as vector rigs like the
> robot (see docs/CHARACTER.md once that lands), so mouth shapes can be drawn and animated in code,
> for every companion at once. That makes "Talking Mochi" (Phase 1, item 7) and "True lip-sync"
> (Phase 3) cheaper than estimated here, and no extra art budget is needed for them.


Voice is feasible for Sidefolk, but the two halves are very different in difficulty. Letting the user speak commands is close to solved today. On macOS 26 and later, Apple's on-device SpeechTranscriber makes about three-quarters fewer errors than the legacy recognizer on clean English. It costs nothing, adds nothing to the app's size, sends no audio off the Mac, and appears to cover all ten of Sidefolk's interface languages. Making Mochi answer in a warm, ChatGPT-class voice is also feasible, but not with anything Apple ships: the built-in voices sound robotic out of the box, the better ones need a manual download, and Siri's voices are closed to apps. The quality bar can be met with a bring-your-own-key neural voice (ElevenLabs' Eleven v4 Turbo tops the independent blind Speech Arena and would cost a typical user about $2 a month) or, for English and fully offline, with the small Kokoro model, which listeners rate about level with OpenAI's realtime voice. What cannot be had cheaply is ChatGPT's way of conversing (listening while it talks, saying "mhmm", stopping when interrupted), which needs a paid speech-to-speech session that the founder's ChatGPT subscription cannot pay for. Phase 1 should therefore be push-to-talk (hold a key, or press and hold Mochi), a live transcript that feeds the existing Ask pipeline unchanged, and short spoken replies only to spoken requests, through swappable voice engines: roughly seven to nine developer-weeks, with the listening half shippable on its own after three to four. A wake word, a live conversation mode, real lip-sync and offline non-English commands should wait, and always-on listening, reusing subscription logins, and APIs that are deprecated or shutting down should not be built at all.

## Understanding spoken commands works on the Mac today, for free and offline

Speech recognition stopped being the hard part sometime in the last eighteen months. Apple's SpeechAnalyzer framework, introduced at WWDC25, ships a new Apple-built SpeechTranscriber model that already powers Notes and Voice Memos. It transcribes **entirely on device**, and its model files live in system storage, so they **add nothing to the app's download size or memory** ([WWDC25 session 277](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/277/)). In an independent LibriSpeech test on an M2 Pro it scored **2.12% word error rate on clean speech and 4.56% on noisy speech**, against 9.02% and 16.25% for the legacy SFSpeechRecognizer and 3.74% and 7.95% for Whisper Small ([GIGAZINE](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260714-apple-speech-analyzer-benchmark/)). That is about 76% fewer errors than the old recognizer and about 43% fewer than Whisper Small. On harder conversational audio it looks less dominant: on English earnings calls, Argmax measured 14.0% error for SpeechTranscriber, better than Whisper base (15.2%), worse than Whisper small (12.8%) and behind NVIDIA Parakeet (11.7%), though Argmax sells a competing SDK ([Argmax](https://www.argmaxinc.com/blog/apple-and-argmax)). Every public number is for English. Short desktop commands spoken close to a laptop microphone sit nearest the clean-speech case, and there Apple's free engine is excellent.

For a companion, speed matters more than leaderboard rank, and the interaction design saves more time than the model does. SpeechTranscriber streams rough partial results almost as words are spoken, then settles each stretch of audio into one final result ([WWDC25 session 277](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/277/)). One developer measured **0.3–0.5 s to the first partial** on a warmed-up iPhone, while an early beta report cited more than 14 s when the model had not been preloaded ([simple_memo, dev.to](https://dev.to/simple_memo/ios-26s-speechanalyzer-on-a-live-mic-the-5-things-the-docs-dont-tell-you-2ng5)); no one has published how long the final result takes on a Mac, so Sidefolk must measure it. The bigger saving comes from push-to-talk. An open-mic assistant has to guess when the user has finished, and fixed 500–1000 ms silence timeouts either cut people off or leave dead air ([Decagon](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-semantic-turn-detection)). When the user releases a key, the turn is over with no guessing, and Sidefolk's no-AI command parser then handles reminders, notes and watches in milliseconds. A spoken "remind me at five to call Sam" can therefore be understood and confirmed aloud in well under a second, faster than the **0.798 s turn-taking latency** OpenAI reports for GPT-Live-1, its fastest voice model ([OpenAI Developer Community](https://community.openai.com/t/introducing-gpt-live-1-in-the-api/1396471)). Questions sent to an AI cannot win that race, because Sidefolk's own decisions note that CLI brains take seconds to start, which is a reason to present voice as a fast way to give commands rather than as a conversation partner.

Apple's language coverage happens to match Sidefolk's ten interface languages well. Apple publishes no official locale list, and counts in the wild range from 10 languages at launch to 42 locales, but a 42-locale printout of `SpeechTranscriber.supportedLocales` from the iOS 26 beta includes **all ten**: English, French, Spanish, German, Italian, Brazilian Portuguese, Japanese, Korean, Simplified Chinese and Arabic ([Anton Gubarenko](https://antongubarenko.substack.com/p/ios-26-speechanalyzer-guide); [Argmax](https://www.argmaxinc.com/blog/apple-and-argmax)). Arabic is the least certain, because shorter lists leave it out, so the app must ask for supported locales at runtime rather than hard-code them. Two constraints follow. There is **no automatic language detection**, since each transcriber is created for a single locale ([Forasoft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/speech-recognition-with-neural-networks-on-ios-1621)), so Sidefolk should listen in its interface language by default and let bilingual users pin a second one. And the no-AI parser stays English-only by decision D28, so a spoken French request goes to the user's AI if one is set up; without AI, the transcript still lands in the Ask box, where the Save, Remind me… and Watch buttons already work in every language. Apple's on-device Foundation Models framework is the natural later fix for non-English commands: it needs macOS 26 with Apple Intelligence turned on, runs privately and free, and covers 16 languages including nine of Sidefolk's ten (not Arabic), so it could turn a non-English transcript into the same structured commands the English parser produces ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/121115); [WWDC26 session 241](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2026/241/)).

Apple's recognizer has three weak spots. It accepts **no custom vocabulary**, so "Mochi", collection names and extension names cannot be boosted; the older DictationTranscriber accepts up to about 100 hint phrases and can handle short commands where that matters ([Apple: DictationTranscriber](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/speech/dictationtranscriber); [Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/speech-framework-vs-sfspeechrecognizer)). It **requires macOS 26**, while Sidefolk supports macOS 15, where the only built-in option is SFSpeechRecognizer forced on device, with about four times the error rate, a one-minute audio limit and a second permission prompt ([Apple: SFSpeechRecognizer](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/speech/sfspeechrecognizer)). And it is **hardware-gated**: it reports itself unavailable on A13 iPhones, M1 and M4 Macs work, and Intel Macs are untested ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/806765)). The strongest third-party alternative is NVIDIA's Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 run through FluidAudio, an Apache-licensed Swift package. On an M4 MacBook Air it scored **2.50% error and 0.10 s average latency per utterance**, against 2.89% and 0.68 s for WhisperKit's large-v3-turbo ([dev.to benchmark](https://dev.to/konrad_sierzputowski_c86a/how-i-benchmarked-on-device-speech-to-text-on-apple-silicon-whisper-vs-parakeet-dne)); it detects the language automatically across 25 European languages, runs on macOS 15, and its CC-BY-4.0 licence requires a credit in the app ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3); [FluidAudio](https://github.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio)). It misses Japanese, Korean, Chinese and Arabic and needs a model download of several hundred MB, which makes it the right Phase 2 add-on for macOS 15 users and European multilingual users rather than the Phase 1 default. Cloud transcription is cheap (OpenAI's gpt-transcribe costs $0.0045 a minute, about $0.34 a month for a typical user ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing))), but short commands give no reason to send the user's voice off the Mac.

The practical traps are in handling microphone audio, not in the models. Microphone audio in the wrong sample format "compiles and runs but produces no transcription, with no error" ([simple_memo](https://dev.to/simple_memo/ios-26s-speechanalyzer-on-a-live-mic-the-5-things-the-docs-dont-tell-you-2ng5)), and live microphone input failed with an error on macOS 26.3 while file input worked ([mac-transcriber research note](https://github.com/yrocaz/mac-transcriber/blob/main/docs/research/2026-07-27-apple-speechanalyzer-docs.md)); macOS 27 adds a ready-made microphone input that removes the hand-written conversion step, but only on 27 ([Apple: Speech updates](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/updates/speech)). Opening an AirPods microphone switches Bluetooth to a low-quality call mode that degrades playback, and in a shipping Mac dictation app, switching audio devices caused format mismatches and repeated restarts ([macparakeet issue #481](https://github.com/moona3k/macparakeet/issues/481)), so Sidefolk should prefer the built-in microphone and rebuild its audio setup whenever the device changes. The app needs `NSMicrophoneUsageDescription` plus the hardened-runtime audio-input entitlement, and a missing usage string gets the app killed the first time it touches the microphone ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfoundation/requesting-authorization-to-capture-and-save-media)). Whether SpeechAnalyzer also triggers the speech-recognition prompt is disputed; two 2026 developer reports say microphone permission alone is enough ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/speech-framework-vs-sfspeechrecognizer)). Sidefolk's own macOS notes already flag that ad-hoc signing changes the app's signature on every build, so microphone permission will reset during development unless dev builds are signed with the Apple Development identity that already exists.

## A warm voice needs a neural voice model, which Apple does not provide

Speaking back is harder, because the founder's bar (natural, warm, not robotic) rules out what macOS gives every app for free. AVSpeechSynthesizer offers compact, enhanced and premium voices, but **only compact voices come installed**; the user has to download the better ones in System Settings ([Apple: voice quality](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avspeechsynthesisvoicequality)), one vendor puts them at roughly 300–600 MB each ([aidictation](https://aidictation.com/blog/text-to-speech-mac)), and no API lets an app start the download. Siri's voices "are not available through the AVSpeechSynthesizer API" ([WWDC20 session 10022](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10022/)). WWDC26 brought expressive, adjustable voices, but only to Siri and only on M3-or-later Macs with at least 12 GB of memory ([9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/06/08/youll-need-the-latest-apple-devices-to-customize-the-new-siris-voice/)), and a review of the macOS 27 betas found the built-in voices "largely unchanged" and less expressive than neural alternatives ([Speech Central](https://speechcentral.net/2026/06/16/ios-27-text-to-speech-a-disappointing-year-for-apple-voices/)). Personal Voice lets apps speak with a copy of the user's own voice after permission, but Apple aims it at people who cannot speak ([WWDC23 session 10033](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10033/)), and a rabbit talking in its owner's voice is the wrong character. Apple's synthesizer also needs the whole text before it speaks ([Picovoice](https://picovoice.ai/blog/ios-streaming-text-to-speech/)), so answers that arrive piece by piece must be cut into sentences anyway. Apple voices are therefore a fallback, offered only when an enhanced or premium voice is installed for the language; a compact voice should never be presented as Mochi's voice.

Independent blind listening tests show how far voice models have come. On the Artificial Analysis Speech Arena, ElevenLabs' **Eleven v4 Turbo ranks first (Elo 1335)** and Eleven v4 second (1330), followed by Alibaba's Qwen TTS, Cartesia Sonic 3.6 (1289) and Google's Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS (1282); OpenAI's own GPT-Realtime-2 voice scores 1075 and its tts-1-hd 1104 ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard)). Turned into win rates with the standard Elo formula, listeners would pick Eleven v4 Turbo over OpenAI's realtime voice about **four times in five**. The surprise is at the low end of the useful range: the tiny, Apache-licensed **Kokoro model scores 1063, effectively tied with OpenAI's realtime voice** (it would win about 48% of comparisons), and through FluidAudio it generates a 3–4 second sentence in **36–54 ms on the Mac's Neural Engine** ([FluidAudio](https://github.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M)). Two caveats keep this honest. The arena rates isolated clips, not conversation, and ChatGPT's current GPT-Live voices are not on the board, so "sounds better than ChatGPT" cannot be claimed. And FluidAudio's Kokoro supports **American English only**, so no local voice of this quality exists yet for Sidefolk's other nine languages.

"Comparable to ChatGPT's voice mode" really means two things. The first is the sound: whether the voice itself is warm and lively, and the arena shows that a chain of recognizer, AI and top-tier voice model meets that bar today. The second is the way it converses. ChatGPT's GPT-Live listens while it speaks, says "mhmm" and adjusts when talked over ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/chatgpt-can-now-listen-and-talk-at-the-same-time-making-ai-conversations-seem-more-human/)), and only a speech-to-speech model can do that. The founder's ChatGPT subscription cannot pay for it: OpenAI bills ChatGPT and the API separately ([OpenAI Help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8156019-is-api-usage-included-in-chatgpt-plus)), and ChatGPT Voice exists only inside OpenAI's own apps and Codex ([ChatGPT Voice docs](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/features/voice)). Reusing Codex's login tokens to reach it would be an undocumented back door, against OpenAI's terms and against Sidefolk's rule never to touch `~/.codex`. Anthropic offers no speech API at all, and its own cookbook pairs Claude with ElevenLabs ([Claude cookbook](https://platform.claude.com/cookbook/third-party-elevenlabs-low-latency-stt-claude-tts)), so for users whose AI is Claude, Sidefolk's core audience, a third-party voice is unavoidable.

### Speech-to-speech gives ChatGPT's conversation style but puts a second party in control

OpenAI now sells the ChatGPT voice engine itself. **gpt-live-1 costs $0.05 per connected minute**, billed per second, and can hand reasoning and tool calls to the developer's own model or agent ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing); [OpenAI Developer Community](https://community.openai.com/t/introducing-gpt-live-1-in-the-api/1396471)). In principle that fits Sidefolk's design, because the voice layer has no tools of its own and every action would still go through Sidefolk's permission layer ([CellCog](https://cellcog.ai/blog/gpt-live-1/)). Early users report inconsistent accents in French, voices that "lack the character" of earlier ones, unreliable call endings and stalls during lookups ([OpenAI Developer Community](https://community.openai.com/t/introducing-gpt-live-1-in-the-api/1396471)). Gemini 3.8 Live is the cheap alternative, at roughly $0.005 a minute in and $0.018 out, with tool calls ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [Gemini 3.8 Live](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/models/gemini-3.8-live)). Either way, two systems would share control: the vendor decides when a turn ends and what to say while the AI thinks, and Sidefolk decides what is allowed. A session left open by mistake also runs up a bill, since eight connected hours of gpt-live-1 cost about $24 a day. This is a Phase 3 option for users who want to chat, not the foundation.

Cost barely separates the realistic options. For a typical user who speaks 30 five-second commands and hears 20 fifteen-word replies a day, Eleven v4 Turbo at $0.04 per thousand characters costs about **$2.04 a month**, Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS about **$0.81** (doubling to $1.62 on 2027-01-01), Cartesia's $5 Pro plan covers recognition and speech together, and on-device Kokoro and Apple recognition cost nothing ([ElevenLabs pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api); [Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [Cartesia pricing](https://cartesia.ai/pricing)). A user ten times heavier would pay about $20 a month with ElevenLabs and $8 with Gemini. Speech-to-speech costs more: the same user, at about 225 connected minutes a month, would pay roughly **$11 for gpt-live-1** before AI charges and **$1.50 for Gemini 3.8 Live**. The choice should rest on quality, latency, privacy and vendor stability, not price.

Privacy needs the most care, because a cloud voice sees text that never needed to leave the Mac. If the local parser saves the reminder "Send the deck to Karim" and Mochi reads it back through ElevenLabs, a reminder that never touched an AI provider has now been sent to a voice vendor that **keeps request history by default** and offers zero retention only on Enterprise plans ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/developers/resources/zero-retention-mode)). Gemini's free tier uses inputs to improve Google's products while the paid tier does not ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)), and OpenAI does not train on API data but keeps realtime audio for 30 days of abuse monitoring ([OpenAI: Your data](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data)). A local-first product should make the on-device voice the default, make any cloud voice an explicit choice that says what gets sent, and offer an "on-device voice only" switch. Licence duties are light but real: OpenAI requires telling users the voice is AI-generated ([OpenAI TTS guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/text-to-speech)), ElevenLabs library voices carry a commercial licence while cloning needs the owner's consent ([ElevenLabs voice guide](https://elevenlabs.io/voice-guide)), and Kokoro needs only an Apache licence notice.

How fast vendors change is the strongest argument for swappable voice engines. Hume's speech and voice APIs shut down on 2026-11-13 ([Hume](https://dev.hume.ai/voice/changelog)); OpenAI's tts-1, tts-1-hd and gpt-4o-mini-tts shut down on 2027-01-06, and their replacement does not work with the same speech endpoint ([OpenAI TTS guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/text-to-speech)); Gemini 3.8 speech and Live prices double on 2027-01-01; Picovoice ended its free wake-word tier on 2026-06-30 ([Home Assistant forum](https://community.home-assistant.io/t/fyi-picovoice-confirmed-free-tier-accesskeys-will-stop-working-after-june-30-2026/1012744)); and Eleven v4 itself only launched on 2026-09-28 ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/eleven-v4)). Sidefolk should treat voices the way it already treats AI providers: one small interface, several engines, each with its own Keychain entry and a "Test" button that speaks one real sentence.

## Push-to-talk wins on trust, speed and accessibility at once

The voice input that people actually use daily on the Mac in 2025–2026 is a hotkey, not a wake word or an open microphone. Wispr Flow's two modes are hold a key to talk and double-tap for hands-free ([Wispr Flow docs](https://docs.wisprflow.ai/articles/2612050838-supported-unsupported-keyboard-hotkey-shortcuts)). Claude Desktop on Mac starts and stops dictation with Caps Lock, ships it **off by default**, and shows the words as they are recognized so the user can check them before clicking send ([Claude Help Center](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/12626668)). ChatGPT's open microphone has drawn a push-to-talk request that has stayed open from May 2025 to October 2026, from users who get cut off when they pause to think and who ask for a "walkie-talkie" mode with a global shortcut ([OpenAI Community](https://community.openai.com/t/urgent-voice-mode-needs-push-to-talk-feature-to-prevent-interruptions/1266130)); earlier Mac reports described ChatGPT hearing its own voice and cutting itself off in a loop ([OpenAI Community](https://community.openai.com/t/voice-mode-not-working-in-mac-app-cutting-off/1245791)). A Digital Trends editor who calls himself "voice-pilled" still holds the fn key rather than talking continuously, and still feels embarrassed dictating in cafés and co-working spaces ([Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/help-im-talking-to-my-computer-its-remarkably-convenient-and-utterly-embarrassing/)). Older research found that people distrust voice assistants with high-stakes actions, and one user compared an unwanted word in a dictated work email to holding "a live grenade" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/voice-assistant-attitudes/)).

That evidence points to one input design. The user holds a shortcut, or presses and holds Mochi, to talk, and releases to send; a double-tap starts a hands-free session for long requests with a clear stop and a timeout, and Esc cancels at any point. The shortcut must be changeable, and the defaults should avoid Fn (Wispr) and Caps Lock (Claude Desktop); Sidefolk's own macOS notes show that two apps can register the same shortcut without either being told, so a clash cannot be detected and must be shown during onboarding. The microphone is on only while the key is held, which turns the orange dot macOS shows whenever an app uses the mic ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-ge/guide/macbook-air/aside/apd8a8f14de5/mac)) into an honest indicator, and Mochi's existing listening pose must be triggered by the same code that turns the microphone on, so the character can never look idle while listening. The words appear live in the Ask box, and the existing hint line updates as the user speaks to show what releasing will do and whether it uses AI. Release can send right away because everything the local parser does already has Undo, and anything with side effects still goes through the permission layer and its approval cards, so voice is never the only confirmation for a risky action. Voice requests join the same history as typed ones, kept as text and marked as spoken, and the audio is thrown away immediately.

The output rules follow the same reasoning. Mochi speaks aloud only to answer a request the user just spoke; typed requests get text, as today. A spoken reply should pass Amazon's "one-breath test", short enough to say in one breath with detail left to the screen ([Amazon Alexa design guide](https://developer.amazon.com/fr/designing-for-voice/what-alexa-says/)), and Sidefolk's voice guide already caps bubbles at about twelve words. For an AI answer, the AI should return one short sentence to speak plus the full card; Mochi never reads URLs, file paths or code aloud, and for lists it says the count and the first item. Interruption has to be instant: pressing the talk key, clicking Mochi or pressing Esc stops the audio, cancels queued speech and any action not yet run, and records that the reply was cut off, since a stale answer arriving afterwards is the main sign of sloppy interruption handling ([Picovoice](https://picovoice.ai/guide/voice-agents/voice-ux-latency-turn-taking/)). Because the microphone is off while Mochi talks, **Phase 1 needs no echo cancellation at all**, and ChatGPT's hearing-itself bug cannot happen. When an AI answer takes a while, Mochi shows its existing thinking pose and says at most one short line that describes what is really happening, such as "Checking with Claude"; chatty filler invites interruptions.

Limiting speech to direct replies also removes most of the "when should Mochi be quiet" problem that unprompted speech would bring. Only three checks remain: Mochi stays silent if another app has the microphone open, which Core Audio can see through the same signal behind the orange dot but misses for Bluetooth headsets ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/741026)); it stays silent if the user has started typing again by the time the answer is ready; and it stays silent in quiet mode, showing the text with a muted-speaker icon. Reading the Mac's Focus status requires an entitlement Apple ties to messaging apps ([Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/682143)), so Sidefolk's own quiet mode stays the main control. Speaking reminders aloud without being asked is a Phase 2 opt-in, because it needs all of these checks and more.

### Mochi should look like it is listening long before it lip-syncs

Duolingo set the high bar for talking characters: each of its characters has more than 20 mouth shapes timed to the individual sounds of speech, layered over blinks and nods that never stop, and the team called making those mouth shapes believable the most critical design task ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes)). Mochi is drawn from 16 pose images rather than an animated rig, so full lip-sync means new art for every talking pose, and neither ElevenLabs nor OpenAI supplies mouth-shape timing with their audio, while Azure does ([Abratabia](https://www.abratabia.com/ai-talking-characters/ai-voice.php); [Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/1183790/code-sample-for-lip-sync)). Phase 1 should instead reuse Mochi's existing listening and thinking poses and add one pair of talking frames (mouth open and closed) switched by how loud the speech is, the same way Mochi's writing animation alternates two frames; the vector robot can simply open and close its existing mouth. Listening feedback matters more than lip-sync: ears up the moment the key goes down, a nod when the request is accepted, and the sad pose plus an editable transcript when nothing was understood do most of the work. The voice must also suit the body. Research finds that when an avatar's voice does not match how it looks, people trust it less and share less ([Cabral et al., Interspeech 2017](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2017/cabral17_interspeech.pdf)), which argues for a small, soft, warm voice that is not childish, matching the voice guide's "no baby talk". Microsoft's Mico is the warning here: it was moved out of Copilot Voice into a narrower tutoring role within ten months of launch ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/2236741/mico-microsofts-weird-lil-ai-guy-has-been-demoted)). Mochi should animate because something real is happening, not to perform.

### Accessibility and privacy follow from the same choices

Voice helps accessibility only if it is never the only way to do something. Every voice action already has a typed equivalent in Sidefolk, and every spoken word should also appear as text in the bubble, which acts as captions by default. When VoiceOver is running, Mochi must not talk over it, and its speech should either adopt the screen reader's voice settings (`prefersAssistiveTechnologySettings`) or go through accessibility announcements ([WWDC20 session 10022](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2020/10022/)); Mochi's view already announces "Mochi is listening", and reduced motion is already respected. Holding a key to talk suits people who pause, stutter or speak slowly, because they decide when the turn ends. Recognition is still much worse for atypical speech: one study measured **36.3% word error** on speech from people with dysarthria or Parkinson's, falling to 23.7% after fine-tuning ([JSLHR 2024](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/full/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-24-00122)), which is another reason to always show the transcript before acting. macOS Voice Control users also need Mochi's controls to carry accessibility labels and a talk shortcut that does not clash with Voice Control's own.

On privacy, the cautionary tales are all about microphones that listen unprompted: Apple's proposed $95M settlement over accidental Siri activations ([Dark Reading](https://www.darkreading.com/cyber-risk/apple-offers-95m-to-settle-siri-privacy-lawsuit)), criticism of Wispr Flow for shipping with its privacy mode off by default ([Voibe](https://www.getvoibe.com/resources/wispr-flow-linkedin-dictation-analysis/)), and the always-listening Friend pendant, which became a social taboo and still struggled to hear ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/10/03/friend-ai-necklace-review-avi-schiffmann/)). Sidefolk's defaults answer all three: the microphone is on only while the key is held, recognition runs on the Mac, text is kept and audio is not, voice data is never used to improve anything, and cloud voices are opt-in with plain disclosure.

## The real risks are vendor churn, macOS audio quirks and misplaced trust

Nothing in this plan is limited by compute or money. Apple's recognizer runs outside the app's memory, Mochi already measures about 0% CPU when idle, and the cloud voices cost a few dollars a month. The risks lie elsewhere, and several are already happening.

| Risk | Evidence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Vendors drop, deprecate or reprice voice APIs within months | Hume ends 2026-11-13; OpenAI speech models end 2027-01-06; Gemini prices double 2027-01-01 | Swappable engines; on-device default; never one vendor only |
| Apple's recognizer: uncertain languages, quiet bugs, silent model updates | Locale counts from 10 to 42; macOS 26.3 microphone bug; no Mac latency data | Ask for supported locales at runtime; test on 26.x and 27; measure before launch |
| AirPods and audio-device changes break capture or degrade playback | Call-mode switch; repeated restarts in a shipping app | Prefer the built-in mic; rebuild audio on device change; choose the input in Settings |
| Microphone permission resets between builds, confusing testers | Ad-hoc signature changes each build (Sidefolk's own notes) | Sign dev builds with the Apple Development identity; Developer ID for testers |
| Accidental key presses spend the user's AI quota | Sidefolk's decision D4 already cites accidental quota use | Ignore empty or very short transcripts; local parser first; "Esc to cancel" hint |
| A cloud voice sends local-only content (reminders, notes) to a vendor | ElevenLabs keeps history by default | On-device voice by default; disclosure; "on-device voice only" switch |
| A speech-to-speech session left open runs up a bill | gpt-live-1 ≈ $24 per 8 connected hours | Phase 3 only; hard session caps; visible cost meter |
| A wake word fires by mistake | openWakeWord targets ≤0.5 false wakes/hour, about 5 a day over a 10-hour day ([openWakeWord](https://github.com/dscripka/openWakeWord)) | Off by default; second check against the transcript; Phase 3 |
| Legal: AI-voice disclosure, cloning laws | OpenAI disclosure rule; NO FAKES Act advancing ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/senate-judiciary-committee-advances-legislation-to-protect-name)); EU AI Act transparency said to apply from 2026-08-02 ([VoxBooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/voice-cloning-legal-cases-2026-recap), unverified) | A designed, non-cloned Mochi voice; say "AI voice" in Settings and About |
| Licence traps in open models | Voxtral TTS non-commercial ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/mistralai/voxtral-4b-tts-2603)); openWakeWord's pretrained models non-commercial; Piper engine GPL ([Yaps.ai](https://www.yaps.ai/blog/best-local-tts-models)) | Allow only Apache, MIT, CC-BY; credit Parakeet and Pocket TTS in About |
| Low use, or users expecting ChatGPT-style chat | Embarrassment in public; GPT-Live raised expectations | Present voice as "quick commands, quick answers"; no claims of conversation |

Several unknowns can only be settled on real Macs, and they belong in the first week of Phase 1 rather than after launch: how long Apple's recognizer takes to give its final result on M1 and M4 Macs, which locales `supportedLocales` actually returns on macOS 26 and 27, whether SpeechAnalyzer triggers a second permission prompt in a properly signed build, how long Kokoro and ElevenLabs take to start speaking over a real home connection, and how much energy a push-to-talk session uses. None of these has been published, and each one changes a default rather than the plan.

## A three-phase plan: ears now, a better voice next, conversation last

### Phase 1: what ships first, in priority order

The P0 rows make Mochi a good listener and can ship on their own; the P1 rows make Mochi answer aloud in a voice that meets the founder's bar. Estimates assume one experienced Swift developer and include tests with recorded audio, strings for all ten languages, and docs. AI-assisted coding shortens the coding but not the device testing, permission flows or voice tuning.

| # | Priority | Item | What "done" means | Estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | P0 | Push-to-talk capture | Hold the shortcut or press and hold Mochi; Esc cancels; mic usage string and entitlement; audio pipeline that handles format conversion and device changes; built-in mic preferred; dev builds signed with a stable identity | 1–1.5 wk |
| 2 | P0 | On-device recognition | SpeechTranscriber on macOS 26+ with language download progress and live partial results; DictationTranscriber for locales SpeechTranscriber lacks; on macOS 15, the older recognizer forced on device and labelled "basic" | 1–1.5 wk |
| 3 | P0 | Speech goes into the normal Ask flow | Live words in the Ask box; hint line previews what releasing will do; release sends through the existing parser or AI, with the same cards, approvals and Undo; history keeps text only | 0.5–1 wk |
| 4 | P0 | Mochi listens | Listening pose driven by the code that turns the mic on; thinking pose while working; sad pose plus an editable transcript when nothing was understood | 2–3 days |
| 5 | P1 | Spoken replies to spoken requests | One shared speech-output interface; sentence splitting for streamed answers; playback queue; instant interruption that cancels queued speech and pending actions; one-breath spoken line; never reads URLs or code; stays silent if another app holds the mic, the user is typing again, or quiet mode is on | 1–1.5 wk |
| 6 | P1 | Voices that meet the bar | Kokoro on device for English (opt-in download showing its size, deletable) as the default; ElevenLabs Eleven v4 Turbo with the user's own key (Keychain, streaming, one chosen Mochi voice) for natural speech in every language; an Apple voice offered only when an enhanced or premium voice is installed | 1.5–2 wk |
| 7 | P1 | Talking Mochi | Two talking frames switched by speech loudness for Mochi (and Spark); the robot's existing mouth opens and closes | 3–5 days including art |
| 8 | P1 | Settings, disclosure, accessibility | Voice settings page (input, shortcut, languages, engine, voice, speed, volume); plain statement of what each engine sends; spoken text always shown; Mochi defers to VoiceOver; STATUS and DECISIONS entries | 1 wk |

The P0 rows add up to about **3–4 developer-weeks** and the P1 rows to about **4–5**, so Phase 1 is roughly **7–9 developer-weeks**.

### Phase 2: worth doing once Phase 1 shows people use voice

| Item | Why it waits | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Better recognition add-on: Parakeet TDT v3 via FluidAudio | Gives macOS 15 users near-Apple accuracy, 0.1 s results and automatic detection across 25 European languages; needs a download and a credit screen | 2 wk |
| Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS as a second cloud voice | Cheapest high-quality voice; reuses a Gemini key the user may already have; still in preview, and the free tier trains on data | 1 wk |
| Non-English commands without an AI provider, via Apple Foundation Models | Turns French or Japanese speech into the same structured commands, privately; needs macOS 26 with Apple Intelligence; no Arabic; prompts must be retested after each OS update | 2–3 wk |
| Custom words | Hint phrases for "Mochi", collection names and extension names, plus a correction pass after recognition | 0.5–1 wk |
| Opt-in spoken reminders and agent updates, per category | Needs the full set of "stay quiet" checks: mic in use, headphones-only option, quiet hours, typing, screen sharing | 1.5–2 wk |
| Hands-free mode with automatic end-of-turn detection and echo cancellation | Talking over Mochi needs echo cancellation (FluidAudio's is in beta) and careful turn detection | 2 wk |

Phase 2 totals about **9–11 developer-weeks** and can be done in pieces, in any order.

### Phase 3: complicated, optional, and decided by usage data

| Item | Why it is hard | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Live conversation mode on gpt-live-1, handing work to Sidefolk's AI (or Gemini 3.8 Live) | Two systems share control; reported call-ending and tool-flow bugs; costs per minute; needs session caps and a cost meter; a conversation-first mode runs against decision D5's "results, not transcripts" | 4–6 wk |
| "Hey Mochi" wake word, opt-in only | Picovoice costs about US$6,000 a year ([Picovoice](https://picovoice.ai/checkout/)); the realistic route is a custom model trained with openWakeWord's method on synthetic Kokoro clips, or sherpa-onnx's text-defined keywords ([sherpa-onnx](https://k2-fsa.github.io/sherpa/onnx/kws/index.html)), with the transcript as a second check; false wakes and the always-on orange dot are product costs | 3–5 wk plus tuning |
| True lip-sync | 6–10 mouth shapes for each talking pose, for each sprite companion; timing taken from the voice model or computed afterwards; per-language mouth mapping | 2–3 wk code plus art |
| A signature Mochi voice | ElevenLabs Voice Design or a blend of Kokoro voices, so Mochi sounds alike across engines and languages; a brand decision as much as engineering | 1–2 wk plus brand work |

### What Sidefolk should not build

| Do not | Why |
|---|---|
| Always-on listening or a wake word on by default | Orange dot on all day, about five false wakes a day, Siri-style legal exposure, no sign that desktop users want it |
| Use the ChatGPT subscription or Codex's login for voice | No sanctioned path; an undocumented back door; breaks the `~/.codex` rule and "no side doors" |
| New work on OpenAI's `/v1/audio/speech`, on Hume, or on Picovoice | Shut down on 2027-01-06; shuts down 2026-11-13; about US$6,000 a year with no free tier |
| Apple compact voices or Personal Voice as Mochi | Robotic; the user's own voice is the wrong character and is meant for people who cannot speak |
| Cloning any real person's voice, including "make Mochi sound like me" | Consent and cloning laws; Pocket TTS's licence forbids non-consensual cloning ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/kyutai/pocket-tts)) |
| Non-commercial or GPL voice models; openWakeWord's pretrained models | Not licensed for a product like Sidefolk |
| Voice as the only confirmation for actions with side effects | The "live grenade" failure; approvals stay clicks |
| Storing audio, or using voice data to improve anything | Contradicts local-first; text history is enough |
| Reading long answers, URLs, code or tables aloud | Fails the one-breath test; the screen does that better |
| A second rule-based command parser per language just for voice | The reason D28 deferred it still applies; Foundation Models or the user's AI covers it |

## Conclusion

The research moves the difficulty somewhere unexpected. Recognition and synthesis are now cheap, permissively licensed and nearly interchangeable, and a typical user would pay a couple of dollars a month at most. What is hard, and what will decide whether voice feels good, are the questions only Sidefolk can answer: when Mochi should stay silent, what it should never read aloud, and how to stay honest about when it is listening. Sidefolk is well placed for these. Its fast local parser and its turn-ends-when-you-release design make it quicker than ChatGPT's voice for the commands it handles most, and its permission layer means voice can never act on anything Guard has not approved.

The bigger strategic point is that "comparable to ChatGPT" is the wrong target. ChatGPT's strength is open-ended, full-duplex conversation, and its loudest user complaint is being cut off; Mochi's best version is a small, warm voice that answers quickly, says one thing, and puts the rest on the screen. Ship that first, measure how often people actually hold the key, and let that number decide whether conversation mode, a wake word or lip-sync are ever worth their weeks.
